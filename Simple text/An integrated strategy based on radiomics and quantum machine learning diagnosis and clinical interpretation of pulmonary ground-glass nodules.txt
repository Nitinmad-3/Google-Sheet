Abstract
Purpose  Accurate classification of pulmonary pure ground-glass nodules (pGGNs) is essential for distinguishing 
invasive adenocarcinoma (IVA) from adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) and minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA), 
which significantly influences treatment decisions. This study aims to develop a high-precision integrated strategy 
by combining radiomics-based feature extraction, Quantum Machine Learning (QML) models, and SHapley Additive 
exPlanations (SHAP) analysis to improve diagnostic accuracy and interpretability in pGGN classification.
Methods  A total of 322 pGGNs from 275 patients were retrospectively analyzed. The CT images was randomly 
divided into training and testing cohorts (80:20), with radiomic features extracted from the training cohort. Three 
QML models-Quantum Support Vector Classifier (QSVC), Pegasos QSVC, and Quantum Neural Network (QNN)-were 
developed and compared with a classical Support Vector Machine (SVM). SHAP analysis was applied to interpret the 
contribution of radiomic features to the models’ predictions.
Results  All three QML models outperformed the classical SVM, with the QNN model achieving the highest 
improvements (p < 0.05) in classification metrics, including accuracy (89.23%, 95% CI: 81.54% − 95.38%), sensitivity 
(96.55%, 95% CI: 89.66% − 100.00%), specificity (83.33%, 95% CI: 69.44% − 94.44%), and area under the curve (AUC) 
(0.937, 95% CI: 0.871 - 0.983), respectively. SHAP analysis identified Low Gray Level Run Emphasis (LGLRE), Gray Level 
Non-uniformity (GLN), and Size Zone Non-uniformity (SZN) as the most critical features influencing classification.

Introduction
According to the classification standards set by the 
International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer/
American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Soci
ety (IASLC/ATS/ERS) [1], approximately 20% of lung 
adenocarcinoma cases present as pure ground-glass 
nodules (pGGNs), which include adenocarcinoma in 
situ (AIS), minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA), 
and some invasive adenocarcinomas (IVA) [2]. Although 
pGGNs typically appear as low-density lesions with 
blurred margins on computed tomography (CT) images, 
their biological behavior and pathological characteristics 
vary significantly. AIS and MIA patients generally have 
favorable prognoses and can be managed through fol
low-up or sublobar resection, while IVA patients require 
more aggressive interventions, such as lobectomy [3, 4]. 
Therefore, accurately distinguishing the invasiveness of 
pGGNs based on CT imaging is critical for optimizing 
patient treatment plans, improving clinical outcomes, 
and enhancing prognosis.
However, classifying pGGNs in clinical practice poses 
several challenges. First, the subtle imaging differences 
among pGGNs, especially in the early stages, make it 
difficult to rely solely on subjective interpretation by 
radiologists, leading to potential diagnostic inconsisten
cies that can impact treatment decisions. Furthermore, 
although biopsy can provide more accurate pathologi
cal information, the small size and complex location of 
pGGNs make biopsy technically challenging, increasing 
the risk of misdiagnosis or complications [5]. Addition
ally, tumor heterogeneity means that a local biopsy may 
not fully represent the pathological characteristics of the 
entire lesion [6]. Thus, the need for non-invasive meth
ods to accurately classify pGGNs remains an urgent chal
lenge in clinical imaging diagnostics.
In recent years, radiomics, as an emerging subject, has 
made significant progress in medical imaging analysis 
[7–9]. Using the classification of pGGNs as an example, 
researchers have successfully developed classical Sup
port Vector Machine (SVM) model to differentiate AIS/
MIA, and IVA based on radiomic features extracted from 
the nodules, which have been evaluated through the 
receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, achieving 
an area under the curve (AUC) of over 0.8 [10]. However, 
most existing studies rely on large annotated datasets, 
which are costly and time-consuming to acquire, and 
their performance often diminishes in small-sample set
tings. Furthermore, model interpretability and feature 
robustness under varying imaging conditions remain 
challenging.
Quantum Machine Learning (QML) has demonstrated 
advantages in processing small, high-dimensional data
sets [11–14]. Leveraging quantum superposition and 
entanglement, QML models maintain high classification 
performance even with limited data [15–17]. Promising 
results have been shown in small-sample medical data
sets, including cardiovascular disease and brain tumor 
classification [18, 19]. However, the application of QML 
in pGGNs classification, particularly in combination 
with radiomic features, is still in its infancy and remains 
largely unexplored.
This study introduces an integrated strategy that com
bines radiomics, QML, and SHapley Additive exPlana
tions (SHAP) analysis to address these challenges. In our 
work, radiomic features were extracted from CT images 
and three QML models-Quantum Support Vector Clas
sifier (QSVC) [20], Pegasos QSVC [21], and Quantum 
Neural Network (QNN) [22, 23]-were developed and 
compared with a classical SVM proposed earlier [10] 
for the classification of pGGNs. To increase the clinical 
relevance, we employed the SHAP method [24, 25] to 
interpret the prediction mechanisms of QML models, 
analyzing the contribution of radiomic features to the 
classification task. Furthermore, we introduced a gray-
level visualization approach that qualitatively represents 
feature differences to provide an intuitive understanding 
of key distinctions between representative samples.
Materials and methods
The overall workflow is depicted in Fig.  1.
Data collection
In this study, we retrospectively analyzed the com
plete clinical data and preoperative CT images of 1,610 
patients who underwent surgical resection for pri
mary lung adenocarcinoma at Sir Run Run Shaw Hos
pital between January 2015 and October 2017 [10]. All 
preoperative CT images were re-evaluated one by one 
using the Picture Archiving and Communication Sys
tem (PACS). Patients were excluded based on the fol
lowing criteria: (1) nodules with solid components; (2) 
nodule diameter larger than 3 cm; (3) a history of other 
sample datasets. It offers a promising tool for early, non-invasive lung cancer diagnosis and helps clinicians make 
more informed treatment decisions.

malignant diseases; (4) poor-quality CT images; and (5) 
patients who had undergone thoracic surgical interven
tions, radiation, or chemotherapy. In total, 275 patients 
(72 men and 203 women, age range 25 to 78 years) with 
322 pure ground-glass nodules (pGGNs) were included 
in this study.
All surgical specimens were formalin-fixed and stained 
with hematoxylin-eosin (HE). Two experienced patholo
gists evaluated the slides using a multi-headed micro
scope and discussed the findings until a consensus was 
reached. Based on the 2015 World Health Organization 
(WHO) classification of lung adenocarcinoma, each nod
ule was classified as AIS/MIA (150, 46.6%) or IVA (172, 
53.4%) [26, 27].
All CT images were obtained utilizing multi-detector 
spiral CT scanners (Siemens SOMATOM Definition 
Flash, Siemens FORCE CT, Siemens Sensation 16, Sie
mens Definition AS 40, and GE LightSpeed VCT). Scans 
were performed from the thoracic inlet to the lung bases, 
with patients trained to hold their breath during scan
ning. Images were reconstructed using standard lung 
window settings (mean, −500 HU; width, 1,500 HU) and 
multi-planar reconstruction (MPR) techniques, including 
coronal and sagittal reconstructions, using a post-pro
cessing workstation.
Radiomic feature extraction
Segmentation data from 322 pGGNs were processed 
using 3D-Slicer software [28], with two radiologists 
reviewing and manually adjusting the segmented regions 
of interest (ROIs) when necessary. The voxel resampling 
was set to 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.7 mm3 instead of the commonly 
used 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 to better preserve fine details of 
small pGGNs. The original CT scans had an average spa
tial resolution of approximately 0.7 × 0.7 × 1.2 mm3, and 
resampling to 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.7 mm3 allowed us to main
tain isotropy, minimize interpolation artifacts, and pre
serve nodule morphology. Radiomic features were then 
extracted using Pyradiomics [29] from the standardized 
segmentation data, resulting in 960 features categorized 
into nine groups. The dataset was randomly split into 
training (80%) and testing (20%) cohorts. In the training 
cohort, the contribution value (importance score) of each 
of the 960 radiomic features was calculated using a Ran
dom Forest (RF) algorithm with 4-fold cross-validation 
for the classification of pGGN-like IVA from AIS/MIA. 
All features were subsequently ranked in descending 
order of their importance scores. To determine the most 
informative subset of features, a sequential forward addi
tion approach was employed: the ranked features were 
added one by one as inputs to the SVM classifier, and 
the overall training accuracy was recorded at each step 
in each iteration. It was observed that the top 20 features 
in each ranking provided the most substantial improve
ments to model performance, while lower-ranked fea
tures (with importance scores < 0.01) contributed 
minimally. To ensure robustness and reduce variability, 
only features that consistently appeared within the top 
20 rankings in at least three of the four cross-validation 
folds were retained. This criterion resulted in the iden
tification of nine radiomic features-four from gray level 
co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), four from gray level 
size zone matrix (GLSZM), and one from gray level run 
length matrix (GLRLM), capturing tumor structure and 
voxel spatial distribution [26], as detailed in Fig. 2.

the QSVC, Pegasos QSVC, and QNN. QSVC [20] uses 
quantum feature maps to project classical data into a

complex feature relationships. It optimizes similarly 
to classical SVM, making previously inseparable data 
linearly separable. The quantum kernel, analogous to 
classical kernel functions, computes inner products of

Additionally, we utilized the QNN for classification 
model building. QNN [22, 23] combines the non-linear 
properties of quantum states with the structure of clas
sical neural networks. It typically consists of a quantum 
feature map, an ansatz circuit, and a measurement step 
(Fig. 3). First, the classical input data x is encoded into a 
quantum state |ϕ(x)⟩ via the quantum feature map Uϕ(x) 
from |0⟩. Then, the quantum state undergoes parameter
ized transformations through the ansatz circuit, com
posed of adjustable quantum gates such as rotation gates 
(Rx(θ), Ry(θ), Rz(θ)) and CNOT gates. Finally, a mea
surement is performed on the quantum state, collapsing 
the quantum information back into classical data.
In the experimental setup, the Qiskit [30] noiseless 
simulator was utilized to construct and train the clas
sification models. The dataset was randomly split into 
training and testing cohorts in an 80:20 ratio. The hyper
parameter optimization process for three QML models 
has been further clarified. For QSVC, the regularization 
parameter (C: 0.005, 0.05, 0.5, 5) and stopping tolerance 
(tol: 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001) were explored. Pegasos QSVC was 
optimized over C (1, 10, 100, 1000) and the number of 
training steps (50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300). For the QNN 
model, optimization included ansatz circuits (RealAm
plitudes, EfficientSU2), optimizers (COBYLA, SPSA, 
NELDER_MEAD), and maximum iterations (100, 300, 
500, 1000, 1500, 1600). Grid search was employed with

Additionally, ablation studies were conducted to inves
tigate the impact of different feature map designs, cho

fixed quantum algorithm structure, on the performance

characteristics were compared: the ZFeatureMap, which 
applies only Z-axis rotations and represents the simplest

richer quantum encoding space; and the ZZFeatureMap, 
which not only includes Z rotations but also introduces 
ZZ entanglement gates to capture more complex corre

the influence of quantum gate designs on the expressivity 
of quantum feature space and the classification perfor

Evaluation metrics and statistical analysis
The performance of all QML models was assessed on 
the testing cohort, with AIS/MIA labeled as the nega
tive class (N) and IVA as the positive class (P). Evalua
tion metrics included ROC-AUC [31], which assesses 
overall classification performance, and the Kolmogorov-
Smirnov (KS) statistic [32, 33], which measures the sepa
ration between positive and negative classes. Accuracy, 
sensitivity, specificity, and odds ratio were derived from 
the confusion matrix [34, 35] to evaluate classification 
quality. All metrics were calculated along with their cor
responding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A weighted 
Z-test [36, 37] was used to evaluate AUC differences 
between each QML algorithm and the classical SVM [10]
in cross-study analysis, with Holm-Bonferroni [38] cor
rection applied for multiple comparisons. Specifically, 
adjusted significance thresholds were calculated sequen
tially for each ranked p-value, and a result was considered 
significant if its p-value was less than the corresponding 
adjusted α.

Results
The models’ classification performance was first evalu
ated using ROC curves (Fig. 4a). The AUC, quantifying 
overall model performance, ranges from 0 to 1, with val
ues closer to 1 indicating better classification. A progres
sive improvement in AUC was observed from Pegasos 
QSVC to QSVC, and finally to QNN. Additionally, the KS 
statistic, shown in Fig. 4b, while a larger maximum differ
ence between the KS curves indicates stronger class sepa
ration. Among the three algorithms, QNN achieved the 
highest KS value, with a classification threshold closest to 
the ideal value of 0.5 for binary classification.
Fig. 4c visually compares the evaluation metrics-accu
racy, sensitivity, and specificity-among the QML mod
els, while Table   1 summarizes all metrics, including 
statistical significance tests for AUC values. The results 
show that all three QML models outperform the classi
cal SVM [10] across metrics, with QNN achieving the 
highest improvements: +12.67% accuracy, +14.20% 
sensitivity, and +13.33% specificity. Statistical analysis 
using weighted Z-tests and Holm-Bonferroni correc
tion revealed that both QSVC and QNN significantly 
outperformed the classical SVM [10] in AUC. Among 
three QML models, QNN exhibited the best predic
tive performance, motivating further exploration. Addi
tionally, ablation experiments were conducted on QNN 
performance (Fig. 4d) and it was observed that the ZFea
tureMap consistently achieved higher accuracy, recall, 
specificity, and AUC values compared to PauliFeature
Map and ZZFeatureMap, highlighting its advantage in 
capturing discriminative patterns in this classification 
task.

Discussion
Our study proposes an innovative integrated strategy for 
pGGNs diagnosis, combining radiomics-based feature 
extraction, QML for model training, and SHAP analysis 
for clinical interpretability. This approach addresses chal
lenges associated with small-sample, high-dimensional 
medical imaging datasets and achieves superior diagnos
tic performance while ensuring interpretability.
In recent years, radiomics has exhibited substantial 
clinical utility. On one hand, it has been used to enhance 
upstream image preprocessing steps such as segmenta
tion and registration, where its sensitivity to algorith
mic variability has been demonstrated in both prostate 
magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [42] and atlas-based 
positron emission tomography (PET) workflows [43]. 
On the other hand, it also serves as a powerful down
stream tool for clinical risk stratification, particularly in 
lung cancer, where its integration with deep learning has 
improved malignancy assessment of ground glass nod
ules (GGNs) [44]. Building on these advances, radiomics 
methodologies have also been extended to a wide range 
of other anatomical regions and tumor types, address
ing broader diagnostic and prognostic challenges. For

has demonstrated promising performance for subtype

also highlighted the value of radiomics in diverse tumor 
types and anatomical regions, such as predicting distant 
metastasis in clear cell renal cell carcinoma [50], assess

machine learning methods, such as SVM and random 
forests, face limitations in generalizing when dealing 
with small sample sizes and high-dimensional data [55]. 
While deep learning can extract high-level features, it 
typically requires large-scale training datasets to avoid 
overfitting [56]. In this study, we integrate QML meth
ods with radiomics, using quantum feature mapping to 
enhance data representation [57]. This approach effec
tively captures nonlinear relationships and improves 
the modeling of tumor heterogeneity. Our experimental 
results show that the QML model outperforms classical 
machine learning models in terms of accuracy, sensitivity, 
and specificity, with QNN achieving the best classifica
tion performance. These results highlight the potential of 
QML to provide more accurate predictions of early-stage 
invasive adenocarcinoma, offering clinicians a reliable 
tool for optimizing follow-up and treatment strategies.
The other key advantage of this work is the use of SHAP 
analysis to enhance the interpretability of QML models. 
SHAP provides both global and individual-level insights 
into how radiomic features contribute to classification 
decisions, effectively bridging the gap between abstract 
numerical data and clinical understanding. Among the 
nine key radiomic features identified from the original 
960 through rigorous feature selection, SHAP analysis 
highlighted GLN and LGLRE as the most influential. This 
aligns with previous research, which identified tumor 
heterogeneity and structural complexity as key factors in 
predicting invasive adenocarcinoma [58, 59]. Addition
ally, JEnt and MP showed minimal contributions, further 
validating QMLs ability to prioritize clinically relevant 
features while disregarding less significant ones [60]. 
These findings suggest that QML combined with SHAP 
can effectively identify high-impact features while reduc
ing noise, thereby enhancing both diagnostic reliability 
and clinical interpretability. SHAP force plots further 
deepened our understanding of individual predictions, 
elucidating how specific radiomic features influenced 
classification outcomes. For TP samples, high GLN val
ues and low LGLRE values are more likely to indicate 
invasive lesions, reflecting increased tumor complexity 
and reduced low-density regions, which aligns with the 
biological characteristics of lung cancer progression [61]. 
Conversely, for TN samples, higher LGLRE and Joint 
Energy (JEne) values indicate greater tissue uniformity 
and more regular structure, consistent with the imaging 
characteristics of benign or healthy tissue [62]. This inter
pretability is critical for clinical adoption, as it provides 
transparent and evidence-based predictions that align 
with medical observations. Additionally, the visualiza
tion of gray-level distributions mapped radiomic features 
into intuitive image annotations. TP samples exhibited 
dense high gray-level regions (red), corresponding to 
tumor heterogeneity and high cellular density, while TN 
samples displayed more extensive low gray-level regions 
(green), indicative of tissue uniformity. By integrating 
numerical data with visual insights, this approach bridges 
the gap between computational predictions and medical 
understanding.
Despite these promising results, certain limitations 
remain. The small sample size used in this study, while 
effective for validating QML models, limits the general
izability of the findings. Future research should focus on 
expanding datasets through multi-center collaborations 
to improve robustness and broader applicability [63, 64]. 
Moreover, the extraction of radiomic features is sensitive 
to image quality and scanning parameters, which may 
introduce biases across different imaging environments. 
Standardizing imaging protocols and feature extraction 
methods is essential to minimize variability [65]. Addi
tionally, while gray-level-based visualization methods 
improve interpretability, they are primarily qualitative. 
Integrating advanced deep learning techniques, such as 
Class Activation Mapping (CAM) [66] and Gradient-
weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) [67], 
could enhance feature mapping accuracy and better illus
trate the relationship between radiomic features and their 
medical significance. Lastly, the scalability of QML mod
els is constrained by current quantum hardware limita
tions [68], which must be addressed to enable widespread 
clinical application.
Conclusion
This research establishes a practical foundation for the 
integration of radiomics, QML, and SHAP analysis in 
medical imaging diagnostics. By combining the comple
mentary strengths of high-performance models and 
interpretability, this integrated strategy ensures transpar
ency while maintaining diagnostic accuracy. The find
ings underscore the potential of this approach to address 
challenges in small-sample, high-dimensional datasets, 
paving the way for the development of scalable, inter
pretable, and clinically effective diagnostic workflows. 
Ultimately, this work represents a significant step toward 
advancing personalized medicine and informed clinical
