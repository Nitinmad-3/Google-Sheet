A B S T R A C T
This review systematizes the field of battery health prediction by introducing a novel classification framework for 
State-of-Health (SOH) and Remaining Useful Life (RUL) models. The landscape is categorized into three distinct 
generations: purely data-driven machine learning (Gen. 1), Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PI-ML, Gen. 2), 
and the emerging Quantum Machine Learning (QML, Gen. 3). Based on this taxonomy, a critical benchmark of 
representative models is presented, evaluating the predictive accuracy across recent AI models to guide practi
tioners in model selection. Finally, limitations of current approaches are identified, and a forward-looking 
research roadmap is proposed for Physics-Informed Quantum Machine Learning (PI-QML) as the next evolu
tionary stage. This roadmap details strategies to address persistent challenges in battery health prognostics. By 
establishing a clear framework, providing a comparative analysis, and charting a path for future research, this 
work provides a structured agenda to accelerate the development of next-generation battery health management 
systems.
1. Introduction
The global imperative for sustainable energy systems has catalyzed 
an unprecedented transition toward electrification, positioning lithium- 
ion batteries (LIBs) as critical enablers of next-generation energy storage 
and electric vehicle (EV) technologies. While fossil fuels have histori
cally dominated energy markets, their environmental external
ities—including substantial greenhouse gas emissions of carbon dioxide 
(CO₂), nitrides, and sulfides—pose significant risks to both environ
mental sustainability and human health [1]. The International Energy 
Agency (IEA) reported that the oil sector alone was responsible for 10.7 
billion tons of CO₂ emissions in 2021, representing one-third of global 
annual emissions [2], while the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) 
identified fuel vehicles as contributing 14 % to total CO₂ emissions [3]. 
In response to these escalating environmental challenges, major 
international entities have established ambitious carbon reduction tar
gets, exemplified by the European Union's commitment to achieving 
net-zero emissions by 2050 [4]. This paradigm shift has intensified focus 
on battery health management as a cornerstone technology for reliable, 
safe, and economically viable battery-powered systems.
The proliferation of EVs, encompassing both purely battery-electric 
and hybrid configurations, represents the most visible manifestation of 
this electrification paradigm shift. The historical trajectory of EVs traces 
back to the early 19th century with the emergence of rechargeable lead- 
acid batteries, during which period EV sales remarkably doubled those 
of gasoline-powered vehicles—an achievement yet unmatched in 
contemporary markets [5]. Contemporary EVs leverage advanced LIB 
architectures to deliver superior performance characteristics, including 
instantaneous torque delivery, zero-emission operation during idle 
states, elimination of fossil fuel dependency, and suitability for frequent

1.1. Scope and contribution of this review
Despite significant advances in machine learning applications to 
battery diagnostics, persistent challenges remain in achieving simulta
neous optimization of predictive accuracy, electrochemical interpret
ability, and computational scalability. The growing research attention to 
SOH estimation and RUL prediction, as evidenced by increasing publi
cation statistics in recent years in Tables 1a and 1b, underscores the 
critical importance of these technologies for battery management sys
tems [8]. However, existing literature lacks a comprehensive taxonomic 
framework that systematically categorizes prognostic methodologies 
from purely traditional machine learning to quantum machine learning. 
Furthermore, the field currently lacks a strategic roadmap for inte
grating emerging quantum machine learning paradigms with estab
lished physics-based modelling approaches.
This critical review addresses these gaps by establishing a compre
hensive intellectual roadmap that systematically charts the trajectory of 
prognostics for LIBs. The analysis encompasses data-driven machine 
learning, Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PI-ML), and Quantum 
Machine Learning (QML) approaches. Through systematic bench
marking of representative models across key performance metrics, this 
work provides practitioners with evidence-based guidance for method
ology selection while identifying fundamental limitations that constrain 
current approaches. The review proposes an innovative forward-looking 
research roadmap for Physics-Informed Quantum Machine Learning (PI- 
QML) model, detailing specific strategies to overcome persistent chal
lenges in battery health management systems. By synthesizing current 
knowledge, establishing performance benchmarks, and charting future 
research directions, this work provides a structured framework to 
accelerate the development of next-generation battery health 
prognostics capable of meeting the demanding requirements of 
emerging energy storage applications. To summarize, the contributions 
of this work are organized as follows: 
1. To propose a systematic framework aiming to classify battery health

2. Evolutionary generation of LIB health prediction models
The predictive modelling of LIB health has progressed through 
distinct evolutionary generations, each defined by its approach to inte
grating data and domain knowledge, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The first 
generation is characterized by purely data-driven models, evolving from 
deep learning to recent generative Transformer models. These models 
leverage large datasets to learn complex statistical correlations but often 
operate as “black boxes”, with limited physical interpretability and poor 
generalization under out-of-distribution conditions. To address these 
limitations, a second generation emerged, based on Physics-Informed 
Machine Learning (PI-ML), which embeds electrochemical princi
ples—such as governing differential equations and physical con
straints—into the model architecture or learning objective. This 
approach enhances data efficiency, interpretability, and robustness. The 
third and most recent generation explores the frontier of Quantum 
Machine Learning (QML), an emerging paradigm that exploits quantum 
phenomena such as superposition and entanglement to navigate the 
high-dimensional state spaces and computational complexities inherent 
in battery degradation, offering a potential pathway to solve estimation 
problems intractable for classical systems.
The three-generation taxonomy is delineated by progressively 
increasing integration of physical domain knowledge into predictive 
architectures: Generation 1 (data-driven methods) encompasses purely 
statistical and machine learning approaches that extract patterns 
directly from empirical observations without explicit incorporation of 
electrochemical principles, relying exclusively on correlative relation
ships within historical datasets; Generation 2 (Physics-Informed Ma
chine Learning) represents hybrid architectures that synergistically 
couple mechanistic battery degradation models, such as electrochemical 
impedance representations with learning algorithms through constraint 
enforcement, and loss function augmentation, thereby embedding 
domain-specific invariances and causality while maintaining data 
adaptability; Generation 3 (Quantum Machine Learning) denotes 
emerging computational paradigms leveraging quantum superposition 
and entanglement to capture complex feature space exploration 
inherent in battery prognostics, representing a frontier methodological 
shift in computational substrate rather than knowledge integration 
strategy. This taxonomy prioritizes the degree of physics integration as 
the primary discriminator, secondarily considering computational 
paradigm novelty, thus establishing non-overlapping categories where 
advancement reflects increasing sophistication in balancing empirical 
generalization with mechanistic fidelity.
2.1. Generation 1: Data-driven machine learning models
Data-driven models excel at capturing the complex, nonlinear 
degradation dynamics of lithium-ion batteries without relying on intri
cate electrochemical models [19,20]. The evolution of these techniques 
has progressed from traditional machine learning to sophisticated

generative architectures, with a notable recent emphasis on 
transformer-based models. The landscape of machine learning models 
for battery prediction, key limitations and challenges are outlined 
below: 
a) Traditional Machine Learning Approaches
Traditional machine learning (ML) models, such as support vector 
machines (SVM), random forests (RF), and gradient boosting trees 
(GBT), have been frequently employed for battery life prediction [21]. 
These methods typically operate on a set of handcrafted health in
dicators (HIs) extracted from measurement data, establishing a mapping 
function between these features and the battery's SOH or RUL. While 
effective in certain contexts, their primary limitation lies in the heavy 
dependence on expert-driven feature engineering. This process can be 
time-consuming and may fail to capture the full spectrum of 
degradation-related information embedded in the raw sensor data, often 
struggling with the temporal complexities of long-term battery cycling. 
b) Foundational Deep Learning Models
To overcome the limitations of traditional ML, deep learning (DL) 
models such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and their var
iants—long short-term memory (LSTM) and gated recurrent units 
(GRU)—have been widely adopted. These models are inherently 
designed to process sequential data, enabling them to learn long-term 
temporal dependencies directly from time-series inputs like voltage, 
current, and temperature curves [22]. Concurrently, convolutional 
neural networks (CNNs) have been utilized, often in hybrid 
configurations, for their proficiency in extracting local, salient features 
from raw data segments [23]. Despite their advantages, RNNs suffer 
from issues like vanishing gradients and sequential processing bottle
necks that hinder parallelization, while CNNs have a limited receptive 
field, making it difficult to capture global, long-range dependencies 
effectively. 
c) The Emergence of Transformer Architectures
The transformer architecture, originally developed for natural lan
guage processing, has revolutionized time-series forecasting, including 
battery health prediction. Its core innovation is the self-attention 
mechanism, which allows the model to weigh the importance of 
different time steps in a sequence, enabling the capture of complex, 
long-range dependencies without recurrent processing [24]. This par
allelizable nature significantly improves training efficiency and perfor
mance on long sequences compared to RNNs [25]. Several studies have 
demonstrated that even a standard transformer can outperform tradi
tional DL models in SOH and RUL prediction tasks by leveraging its 
ability to model global correlations within the battery's degradation 
trajectory [26]. 
d) Hybrid and Advanced Transformer Architectures
Recent research has focused on enhancing the standard transformer 
architecture through hybridization and architectural innovations to 
better suit the specific challenges of battery data. Hybrid models inte
grate transformers with other neural network components to leverage 
their complementary strengths. For instance, combining a transformer

layers for initial local feature extraction before the transformer pro
cesses the sequence for global context [29]. Architectural advancements 
have also been proposed to improve efficiency and capability. The 
predictive pretrained transformer (PPT) and other variants employ a 
probabilistic sparse self-attention mechanism to reduce the computational 
complexity from O(L²) to O(L log L), making them more scalable for very 
long sequences [21].
Other novel approaches include the NARX (Nonlinear Auto-Regressive 
with Exogenous inputs) Transformer, which uses its own past predictions 
as feedback to better model dynamic systems [30], and the Inverted 
Token Transformer, which reorients the attention mechanism to focus on 
correlations between different sensor variables (e.g., voltage, current) 
rather than across time steps [31]. These advanced architectures are 
often coupled with signal preprocessing techniques like Savitzky-Golay 
filtering (SGF) or Complete Ensemble Empirical Mode Decomposition 
with Adaptive Noise (CEEMDAN) to denoise the input data and handle 
non-stationarity [25]. 
e) Recent Innovations in Transformer Architectures
A prominent trend is the enhancement of input data representation 
through advanced signal processing and structural hybridization before 
feeding data into the transformer core. Recognizing that raw battery 
data is often noisy and non-stationary due to capacity regeneration, 
researchers have integrated sophisticated preprocessing modules. 
Methods such as Savitzky-Golay filtering (SGF), Complete Ensemble 
Empirical Mode Decomposition with Adaptive Noise (CEEMDAN), and 
Empirical Wavelet Transform (EWT) are employed to decompose the 
time series into more stable trend and fluctuation components [25,31]. 
This data-centric approach provides the transformer with a cleaner, 
more informative input. Furthermore, hybrid architectures incorporate 
modules like a Denoising Autoencoder (DAE) to learn robust features or 
a Segmented Hidden Markov Model (SHMM) to capture discrete state 
transitions in the battery’s degradation process, thereby structuring the 
input to highlight critical aging phases [27,32].
Another key direction has been the architectural modification of 
transformers to improve computational efficiency, a critical step for 
deployment on resource-constrained BMS. The quadratic complexity of 
the standard self-attention mechanism poses a significant barrier. To 
address this, lightweight architectures have been proposed. The Light- 
Term Memory Network (LTM-Net) replaces the computationally inten
sive softmax operation with a linear attention mechanism, significantly 
reducing complexity while incorporating a forgetting gate to manage 
temporal information flow [33]. Concurrently, the Predictive Pretrained 
Transformer (PPT) leverages a probabilistic sparse (ProbSparse) atten
tion mechanism, which selectively computes attention scores for only 
the most significant query-key pairs, achieving near-linear complexity 
[21]. These innovations make transformer-based models more feasible 
for real-time, on-device battery diagnostics.
To combat the challenge of limited labelled data in real-world sce
narios, recent work has focused on advanced learning paradigms like 
semi-supervised and transfer learning. The self-transformer framework, 
for instance, utilizes a small labelled dataset to train an initial model, 
which then generates pseudo-labels for a larger pool of unlabelled data. 
This approach is enhanced with a noise-learning module to improve the 
model's tolerance to inevitable errors in the pseudo-labels [34]. In a 
similar vein, transfer learning has been effectively applied through 
models like the PPT, which is pre-trained on a large, diverse dataset and 
then fine-tuned on a smaller, specific target dataset, adapting its learned 
knowledge to new battery types and conditions with minimal data [21]. 
Additionally, hybrid global-local models fuse a global transformer pre
dictor with a local online correction module, such as an Unscented 
Particle Filter (UPF), allowing a general model to be dynamically 
adapted to a specific battery's unique degradation path in real time [35].

2.2. Generation 2: Physics-informed machine learning models
Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML) for lithium-ion battery 
health prediction is a paradigm that integrates governing electro
chemical and thermal principles into data-driven frameworks to 
enhance the accuracy, sample efficiency, and physical plausibility of 
SOH and RUL estimations. PIML approaches for LIB health prediction 
can be categorized into three primary paradigms as follows: 
a) Physics-Informed Features and Structural Integration
Physics-Informed Features and Structural Integration represents the 
foundational approach where electrochemical models such as Equiva
lent Circuit Models (ECM), Fractional-Order ECM (FOECM), and 
Electrochemical-Thermal-Mechanical-Side reaction (ETMS) models 
extract physically meaningful parameters including internal resistance, 
capacitance, and membrane resistance as inputs to machine learning 
algorithms [42,43]. More sophisticated implementations employ 
Physics-Guided Algorithm Structures (PGAS) through dual-branch 
frameworks 
operating 
data-driven 
branches 
in 
parallel 
with 
physics-informed branches that explicitly model processes such as Solid 
Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) growth, with outputs fused in latent space to 
produce physically plausible predictions [44,45]. Advanced architec
tures utilize Physics-Informed Deep Operator Networks (PI-DeepONet)

b) Physics-Constrained Loss Function Architectures
Physics-Constrained Loss Function Architectures implement Physics-

Representative

Lightweight interpretable physical models (LIPM) based on Lorentzian 
functions generate physical parameters from incremental capacity 
curves that constrain physics-informed neural networks predicting 
voltage-capacity relationships, while residuals of governing ordinary or 
partial differential equations derived from ECM dynamics or 
semi-empirical degradation models serve as penalty terms guiding 
network optimization toward physically consistent solutions [47,50]. 
c) Deeply Integrated Hybrid Architectures
Deeply Integrated Hybrid Architectures construct frameworks where 
physical models serve as functional and differentiable components 
within neural network forward passes, enabling direct enforcement and 
backpropagation through physical laws. Sophisticated implementations 
involve integrating open-circuit voltage (OCV) reconstruction models 
into convolutional neural networks for electrode-level diagnostics, 
where CNNs predict aging parameters subsequently utilized by OCV 
models to generate voltage curves [51]. The Bayesian calibrated PINN 
framework introduces dedicated "physics model calibrators"—deep 
neural networks learning discrepancies between idealized physics 
models derived from porous electrode theory and real-world observa
tions [52]. Physics-Informed Recurrent Neural Networks (PI-RNNs) 
exemplify architectural integration where aging-aware electrochemical 
models are embedded within recurrent cell state update mechanisms, 
with physics-based models providing initial capacity degradation esti
mates subsequently refined by RNN hidden states [53].
2.3. Generation 3: Quantum machine learning models
An emerging research trend in battery prognostics leverages quan
tum computing to address the shortcomings of classical machine 
learning in modelling complex, non-linear degradation dynamics and 
optimizing high-dimensional parameter spaces. The application of 
quantum-inspired and quantum-native algorithms has bifurcated into 
two primary categories for battery state estimation. The first involves 
the use of quantum-inspired metaheuristic algorithms to optimize clas
sical machine learning models, while the second focuses on developing 
inherently quantum or hybrid quantum-classical models that leverage 
quantum mechanics for computation. These approaches aim to mitigate 
issues such as premature convergence to local minima and improve the 
ability to model intricate temporal dependencies in battery health data. 
a) Quantum-Inspired Metaheuristic Algorithms
One prominent approach involves leveraging quantum-inspired 
metaheuristic algorithms to enhance the parameter optimization of 
established classical machine learning frameworks. For instance, the 
Quantum Genetic Algorithm (QGA) has been successfully employed to 
optimize the smoothing factor and weights of a Generalized Regression 
Neural Network (GRNN), improving its adaptive capabilities and esti
mation accuracy [54]. Similarly, Quantum Particle Swarm Optimization 
(QPSO) and its extended variants like EQPSO have been utilized to tune 
the parameters of neural networks and filter models [55,56]. The 
primary objective of these quantum-inspired optimizers is to enhance 
the global search capabilities and maintain a better balance between 
exploration and exploitation, thereby avoiding premature convergence 
to local optima, which is a common challenge in training complex 
non-linear models for battery SOH forecasting. 
b) Quantum Computing-based Learning Models
A second, more fundamental category involves the direct integration 
of quantum computing principles into the core of the learning models 
themselves. A notable example is the Quantum Support Vector Regres
sion (QSVR), which utilizes quantum kernels to map battery health in
dicators into high-dimensional quantum Hilbert spaces, allowing for the 
capture of complex degradation patterns that are intractable for classical 
kernels [57]. Furthermore, hybrid models such as the Evolving Quantum 
Fuzzy Neural Network (eQFNN) and the Extended Kalman Quantum 
Particle Filter (EKQPF) have been proposed, which leverage quantum 
concepts like quantum fuzzy sets for rule adaptation and quantum state 
superposition for particle representation [55,58]. The EKQPF, for 
instance, employs quantum principles and Grover's search algorithm for 
more efficient particle resampling, addressing the particle degeneracy 
problem in traditional filters.
These two categories represent distinct levels of quantum integra
tion, with quantum-inspired optimization being more readily imple
mentable on classical hardware [59]. A prevailing trend is the 
development of hybrid and ensemble frameworks that combine these 
approaches to maximize predictive performance. For example, a 
Quantum-Enhanced Ensemble Model (QEM) may integrate a QSVR in 
parallel with a classical Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) and refine the 
combined output with a Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) layer [57]. 
Such hybrid architectures aim to synergize the enhanced feature map
ping of quantum models with the proven temporal modeling capabilities 
of classical networks like GRUs, providing a robust pathway for 
advancing battery prognostics with higher accuracy and stability.

4.2. Generation 2: Physics-informed machine learning model
The primary limitation of PIML frameworks stems from computa
tional complexity and scalability challenges. Integration of complex 
physical models, particularly partial differential equations governing 
electrochemical processes such as porous electrode theory and thermal 
dynamics, dramatically increases training time and memory re
quirements, creating significant computational bottlenecks [49,65,66]. 
The incorporation of sophisticated electrochemical models into training 
loops requires innovative optimization strategies including decoupled 
training approaches that separate data-driven and physics-informed 
component training, multi-stage sequential optimization beginning 
with physics-based initialization followed by data-driven refinement, 
and adaptive loss weighting schemes to balance data-fitting and 
physics-residual loss contributions [45,64]. Model compression tech
niques including network quantization and pruning become essential for 
deployment on resource-constrained BMS hardware, though these ap
proaches risk compromising the physical interpretability that consti
tutes PIML's primary advantage [67].
Additionally, data scarcity and heterogeneity present persistent 
challenges that fundamentally limit PIML performance and generaliz
ability. Second-life batteries exhibit particularly acute challenges due to 
incomplete first-life usage histories and abrupt operational condition 
shifts, creating significant training data gaps that conventional ap
proaches struggle to address [13,53]. While PIML frameworks demon
strate 
exceptional 
small-sample 
performance 
by 
leveraging 
physics-based priors that constrain model behaviour with limited 
training data, enabling accurate predictions with as few as one-cycle 
charging curves, data heterogeneity arising from diverse battery 
chemistries specifically Lithium Nickel Cobalt Aluminum Oxide (NCA), 
Lithium Nickel Cobalt Manganese Oxide(NCM), Lithium Iron Phosphate 
(LFP), form factors, and operating conditions poses additional general
izability challenges requiring extensive cross-domain validation [13,50,
68,69]. Transfer learning approaches enable models trained on abun
dant protocol-based cycling data to adapt to sparse real-world usage 
patterns, though effectiveness remains limited by domain shift magni
tudes and underlying physical model fidelity [69].
Furthermore, model complexity and interpretability trade-offs 
constitute a fundamental limitation where increasing physical model 
sophistication often reduces computational efficiency and practical 
deployability. The efficacy of physics-constrained approaches depends 
critically on simplified physical model fidelity and meticulous tuning of 
weighting factors balancing data-driven and physics-informed loss 
components, introducing hyperparameter sensitivity that can compro
mise robustness [50,70]. Multi-task learning scenarios involving 
co-estimation of SOH and RUL require customized gated control models 
to manage information flow between shared and task-specific layers, 
mitigating negative transfer effects and the "seesaw phenomenon" where 
conflicting optimization objectives degrade overall performance [63]. 
Advanced architectures integrating Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks 
(KANs) with PINNs and attention mechanisms provide enhanced inter
pretability but introduce additional computational overhead and 
architectural complexity that may limit practical implementation [71].
4.3. Generation 3: Quantum machine learning model
A primary and consistent challenge of the quantum machine learning 
models is their significant computational overhead and latency, espe
cially when simulated on classical hardware. While QMLs incorporating 
quantum principles can achieve high accuracy, they do so at the cost of 
substantially increased processing time. For instance, the Quantum- 
Enhanced Ensemble Model (QEM) and its Quantum Support Vector 
Regression (QSVR) component required simulation times an order of 
magnitude higher (e.g., ~14.4 s and ~11.7 s on average) than tradi
tional counterparts like Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) (~0.1 s) and 
standard SVR (~1.2 s) [57]. This latency stems from the inherent 
complexity of classically simulating quantum phenomena such as su
perposition and entanglement, as well as the overhead associated with 
encoding classical data into quantum states and subsequently decoding 
the results, a process which is identified as time-consuming [55]. This 
high computational demand currently limits the practical deployment of 
these models in real-time battery management systems where rapid 
inference is critical.
Beyond computational cost, QML models exhibit challenges related 
to robustness, noise sensitivity, and generalization. The quantum com
ponents can introduce significant variability and instability into pre
dictions. This is explicitly addressed in QEM model [57], where a 
classical GPR layer was strategically added to the QEM specifically to 
smooth out the quantum fluctuations and mitigate the inherent quantum 
variability originating from the QSVR model. Furthermore, some 
quantum architectures, particularly those based on deep quantum cir
cuits, introduce numerous tunable variational parameters, which can 
lead to over-parametrization. This makes the model highly specific to 
the training dataset and hinders its ability to generalize to new, unseen 
data, a critical flaw for reliable state-of-health forecasting. This inherent 
stochasticity contrasts with the deterministic nature of many traditional 
ML models and the constraint-driven stability of physics-informed 
models.
5. A forward-looking research roadmap for physics-informed 
quantum machine learning (PI-QML)
To address the key limitations and challenges in standalone PIML 
and QML, this review presents a research roadmap for developing 
PIQML models, outlining seven novel directions that synergistically 
integrate quantum computing with electrochemical physics constraints, 
as summarized in Fig. 4. The goal is to enhance battery health prediction 
accuracy and interpretability, specifically by leveraging quantum 
advantage to navigate the complex optimization landscapes and high- 
dimensional parameter spaces inherent in battery degradation model
ling with physics constraints. The key seven novel directions are pro
posed as follows:
5.1. Physics-Informed Variational Quantum Circuits for enhanced model 
accuracy and interpretability
While traditional PIML is powerful, they struggle to accurately 
represent the complex, non-local correlations inherent in electro
chemical systems [45,52]. This representational gap can lead to models 
converging on physically implausible local minima during optimization, 
thereby limiting their predictive accuracy for complex phenomena like 
battery degradation [54]. To address this challenge, a hybrid framework 
that embeds Variational Quantum Circuits (VQCs) [72] as expressive 
with physics-informed constraints is proposed by leveraging quantum 
entanglement from VQC to naturally capture the intricate, long-range 
dependencies between electrochemical parameters that are often 
missed by classical layers, complemented by physics constraints to 
model battery degradation mechanism. In other words, the VQC ansatz 
is explicitly designed to be interpretable by encoding fundamental 
conservation laws and degradation mechanisms, such as SEI growth and 
lithium plating. By hard-coding these physical principles into the cir
cuit's structure, the VQC acts as a powerful inductive bias. This provides 
a strong regularization effect that constrains the solution space, guiding 
the optimization process away from non-physical outcomes and toward 
solutions with higher fidelity. The result is a model with enhanced

directly reflects the underlying physics of the system it is modelling.
PI-VQCs represent the most viable entry point for NISQ imple

quantum structure allows the VQC ansatz to remain shallow, mini
mizing decoherence effects while leveraging classical optimizers for 
parameter updates. By hard-coding conservation laws and degradation 
mechanisms (e.g., SEI growth) directly into the circuit structure, the 
effective solution space is restricted; this inductive bias not only miti
gates the barren plateau problem common in noisy optimization but also 
reduces the requisite gate count, making the model robust enough for

the constraints of the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era.
5.2. Quantum kernel methods for multi-scale electrochemical modelling
Current PIML frameworks struggle with hierarchical multi-scale 
modelling, where microscopic phenomena like SEI growth must be in
tegrated with macroscopic battery performance metrics, creating 
computational complexity and numerical instability issues [66]. 
Advanced PIML architectures require construction of hybrid frameworks 
where physical models serve as functional components within neural 
network forward passes [58]. Classical quantum kernel methods are 
limited by quantum hardware availability and restricted feature 
mapping capabilities when dealing with high-dimensional battery state 
spaces [73]. The proposed Quantum Kernel Physics-Informed Networks 
(QK-PINs) employ quantum feature maps to encode multi-scale elec

phenomena with macroscopic physics-informed models for pack-level 
behaviour [66], leveraging quantum computational advantages to 
handle exponential complexity of multi-scale interactions.

5.3. Quantum optimization for adaptive loss weighting in multi-physics 
constraints
PIML methods face significant challenges in balancing contributions 
between data-fitting loss and physics-residual loss during training, 
requiring meticulous tuning of weighting factors that often leads to 
suboptimal performance [74]. Adaptive loss weighting schemes must 
dynamically balance contributions to prevent dominance of either 
component. Quantum-inspired metaheuristic algorithms suffer from 
premature convergence when optimizing neural network parameters in 
complex battery modeling scenarios [75]. The Quantum Adaptive Loss 
Weighting Networks (QALWNs) is proposed which employs Quantum 
Approximate Optimization Algorithms (QAOA) to dynamically optimize 
loss weighting schemes during training [76]. The quantum optimizer 
leverages quantum tunnelling effects to escape local minima in 
high-dimensional weighting parameter spaces while simultaneously

constraints 
from 
electrochemical-thermal-mechanical models. The framework in

addressing the computational challenges of multi-stage training ap
proaches [77]. This enables automatic adaptation of loss weights for 
multi-task learning scenarios involving co-estimation of SOH and RUL, 
eliminating manual hyperparameter tuning while preventing the seesaw 
phenomenon where conflicting optimization objectives degrade 
performance.
QAOA-based adaptive weighting exhibits limited near-term viability 
due to the exponential circuit depth requirements for achieving solution 
quality competitive with classical optimizers. While depth, p=1 QAOA 
circuits are NISQ-compatible, they provide insufficient approximation 
ratios (<0.7) for high-dimensional loss weighting optimization in multi- 
physics battery models. Achieving acceptable performance (approxi
mation ratio >0.9) requires p≥10 layers, resulting in circuit depths 
exceeding 1000 gates that surpass current error correction capabilities. 
A practical approach involves co-optimization via a hybrid framework, 
wherein quantum-generated proposals undergo classical refinement 
within an outer optimization loop. Additionally, the requisite number of 
quantum evaluations is minimized through the integration of noise- 
aware ansatz designs and classical surrogate models.
5.4. Quantum-Enhanced uncertainty quantification for battery 
prognostics
PIML methods struggle with robust uncertainty quantification in 
degradation predictions, particularly when dealing with model param
eter uncertainty and aleatory variability in battery aging processes. 
Physics-Informed Bayesian Neural Networks provide uncertainty quan
tification but face computational limitations [52]. QML approaches lack 
sophisticated uncertainty propagation mechanisms that can handle both 
quantum and classical uncertainty sources simultaneously [78]. To 
address this, the Quantum Bayesian Physics-Informed Networks 
(QB-PINs) is proposed to leverage quantum superposition to represent 
probability distributions over physics-constrained model parameters 
[79]. The framework employs quantum amplitude estimation algo
rithms to efficiently compute posterior distributions of degradation pa
rameters while enforcing electrochemical constraints from equivalent 
circuit models. Quantum Monte Carlo sampling techniques propagate 
uncertainties through complex physics-based forward models derived 
from porous electrode theory [80], providing probabilistic forecasts 
with quantified confidence intervals. This approach addresses the limi
tations 
of

by

QB-PINs present significant challenges for current NISQ devices, 
placing them lower on the implementability hierarchy due to their 
reliance on Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE) to compute posterior 
distributions of degradation parameters. Standard QAE requires deep 
circuits that exceed current coherence times; while "shallow" variants 
without phase estimation exist, the propagation of uncertainties through 
complex porous electrode forward models demands high gate fidelities 
to ensure that quantum noise does not overwhelm the aleatory vari
ability and parameter uncertainty being modelled. The near-term 
implementation strategy focuses on iterative Quantum Amplitude Esti
mation (IQAE) and low-depth hybrid variants, strictly applied to small 
parameter subsets. For high-dimensional marginals, these quantum 
routines are integrated with classical Bayesian surrogates, such as con
ditional density estimators. To ensure that quantum estimates remain 
statistically significant on NISQ hardware, rigorous error mitigation 
protocols—specifically Zero-Noise Extrapolation (ZNE) and Probabi
listic Error Cancellation (PEC)—are employed alongside variance 
reduction techniques.

modelling

cell variations and thermal-electrochemical coupling within battery

Multi-fidelity physics-informed approaches struggle with heterogeneous 
data sources and diverse operational conditions [66]. QML methods lack 
specialized architectures for handling graph-structured battery pack 
topologies with physics-informed edge relationships. To bridge this gap, 
a Quantum Graph Physics-Informed Networks (QG-PINs) is introduced 
to represent battery packs as quantum-enhanced graphs where nodes 
encode individual cell states and quantum-weighted edges capture 
thermal-electrochemical interactions. The quantum graph convolutions 
[82] leverage quantum walk algorithms to propagate physics-based 
constraints across pack topology while preserving fundamental conser
vation laws and electrochemical principles. Quantum attention mecha
nisms [83] dynamically weight cell interactions based on thermal 
conductivity, electrical connectivity, and degradation synchronization, 
addressing the computational challenges of multi-fidelity approaches. 
This framework enables accurate modelling of pack-level degradation 
patterns while handling data heterogeneity arising from diverse battery 
chemistries and operating conditions, maintaining computational effi
ciency through quantum parallelism in graph operations.
Quantum graph methods and quantum-walk primitives conceptually 
align with pack-topology problems, but realistic battery packs with 
tens–hundreds of cells produce graphs that exceed the qubit counts and 
connectivity (and thus circuit depth) of current NISQ processors; 
moreover, graph mappings tend to introduce many SWAPs on limited 
topologies, compounding noise. Short-term implementable mitigations 
include hybrid architectures where classical GNN layers operate on the 
global graph and small quantum submodules model critical local motifs 
(e.g., cell pairs or thermal hotspots), as well as compressing graph sig
nals into low-rank summaries before quantum processing.

and heterogeneous aging patterns [17]. The persistent challenge of data 
scarcity requires innovative solutions that leverage limited available 
information while ensuring model generalizability. QML models lack 
mechanisms to enforce hard physics constraints during synthetic data

model classically. This framework addresses the challenge of generating 
large-scale, physically realistic battery lifecycle datasets that preserve 
complex nonlinear degradation patterns, significantly expanding 
training data availability for rare degradation scenarios while main

QG-PCNs face severe implementability constraints on NISQ hard
ware due to the notorious training instability of Quantum GANs 
(QGANs) combined with the difficulty of loading classical battery data 
into quantum states. While the variational generator can theoretically 
capture non-local correlations in aging patterns, the barren plateau 
phenomenon is exacerbated in generative tasks, and the requirement to 
enforce hard physics constraints via a discriminator imposes additional 
circuit depth that currently limits practical validation to extremely low- 
dimensional synthetic datasets rather than realistic electrochemical 
profiles. The iterative adversarial training loop necessitates thousands of 
generator-discriminator evaluations, each involving quantum circuit 
execution, making training times prohibitive on current quantum cloud 
platforms with limited allocation quotas and queue-based access 
models.
5.7. Quantum causal discovery for interpretable degradation mechanism 
identification
Current PIML interpretability approaches are limited to correlation- 
based analysis and cannot identify causal relationships between opera
tional conditions and specific degradation mechanisms, hindering 
development of targeted mitigation strategies [66]. The integration of 
causal discovery algorithms with PIML frameworks represents a frontier 
development for identifying underlying causal relationships [86]. QML 
methods lack frameworks for quantum-enhanced causal inference in 
complex 
electrochemical 
systems. 
The 
Quantum 
Causal 
Physics-Informed Networks (QC-PINs) is proposed to leverage quantum 
algorithms for causal discovery to identify dominant degradation 
pathways 
and 
their 
root 
causes. 
The 
framework 
employs 
quantum-enhanced constraint satisfaction algorithms to evaluate causal 
hypotheses while enforcing physics-based constraints on causal re
lationships derived from electrochemical-thermal-mechanical models. 
Quantum entanglement enables evaluation of multiple causal structures 
involving primary degradation modes including SEI layer growth, 
lithium plating, and active material loss, while quantum interference 
amplifies causal signals and suppresses spurious correlations. This 
approach 
can 
identify 
complex 
causal 
chains 
such 
as 
temperature-dependent lithium plating causally linked to SEI growth 
acceleration under high C-rate condition, providing unprecedented 
diagnostic insights for electrode-level diagnostics [51] and targeted 
battery management optimization strategies that address specific 
degradation mechanisms rather than just predicting overall capacity 
fade.
QC-PINs represent the least NISQ-implementable direction due to the 
nascent state of quantum causal inference algorithms and their extreme 
computational demands. Evaluating causal hypotheses over degradation 
mechanisms requires quantum constraint satisfaction algorithms oper
ating on graph structures with O(2k) nodes for k potential causal vari
ables, quickly exceeding representational capacity of <100 qubit 
systems when considering realistic numbers of operational and 
degradation parameters (temperature, C-rate, SEI thickness, lithium 
plating extent). Quantum interference-based amplification of causal 
signals demands high-fidelity coherent operations across the entire 
quantum register for extended durations incompatible with NISQ 
coherence times. A summary of feasibility of implementation of these

6. Feasibility and practicality of PI-QML in battery SOH and RUL 
prediction
PI-QML presents a highly feasible and practical paradigm for 
enhancing battery SOH and RUL prediction, primarily by addressing the 
inherent complexities of electrochemical systems that challenge clas
sical methods. The integration of VQCs with physics-informed con
straints, as in Physics-Informed Variational Quantum Circuits (PI-VQCs), 
leverages quantum entanglement to capture intricate, long-range de
pendencies between electrochemical parameters. This capability 
directly tackles the representational gap in traditional PIML, preventing 
convergence to physically implausible local minima and thereby 
improving predictive accuracy for phenomena like battery degradation. 
Furthermore, QK-PINs offer a robust solution for multi-scale electro
chemical modelling by encoding molecular-level degradation into high- 
dimensional quantum Hilbert spaces, effectively bridging microscopic 
phenomena with macroscopic performance metrics, which is crucial for 
SOH and RUL assessment. Fig. 6 shows an overview of the challenges 
and proposed implementation solutions to addresses these issues as the 
future directions.
6.1. Issues and challenges
Despite the promising advancements, the practical implementation 
of PI-QML in BMS for SOH and RUL prediction faces significant issues 
and challenges, primarily stemming from the nascent stage of quantum 
computing hardware. The current limitations in quantum hardware, 
including the number of available qubits, their coherence times, and 
error rates, pose substantial hurdles. Algorithms like VQCs and QK-PINs, 
while theoretically powerful, may demand quantum resources beyond 
current capabilities, making it difficult to execute complex quantum 
operations efficiently and reliably within the constrained computational 
environment of an embedded BMS. Furthermore, the inherent sensi
tivity of quantum states to environmental noise necessitates robust error 
correction mechanisms, which themselves introduce additional 
computational overhead and complexity, potentially negating the 
quantum advantage in resource-limited applications such as real-time 
BMS operations.

Another critical challenge lies in the integration of quantum algo
rithms with existing classical BMS infrastructure and the development of 
specialized quantum-classical interfaces. The conversion of classical 
electrochemical data into quantum states, execution of quantum algo
rithms, and subsequent extraction of classical predictions for SOH and 
RUL require sophisticated quantum compilers and runtime environ
ments that are not yet mature. Moreover, the training of PI-QML models, 
particularly those involving quantum optimization (e.g., QALWNs) or 
quantum generative models (e.g., QG-PCNs), is computationally inten
sive and demands significant off-line quantum processing capabilities 
that are not feasible within an operational BMS. The interpretability and 
explainability of quantum-derived predictions, although inherently 
addressed by physics constraints, also present a learning curve for en
gineers accustomed to classical diagnostics, hindering rapid adoption 
and trust in critical safety-related applications.
6.2. Proposed future directions
To address the challenges in practical PI-QML implementation for 
BMS, a multi-faceted approach is required, focusing on hardware- 
software co-design and the development of hybrid quantum-classical 
architectures. Near-term solutions should prioritize the optimization 
of PI-QML algorithms for current Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum 
(NISQ) devices, emphasizing variational quantum algorithms that are 
more resilient to noise and require fewer qubits. This includes devel
oping more compact VQC ansatz, efficient quantum feature maps for 
QK-PINs, and resource-optimized QAOA implementations for QALWNs, 
ensuring that the quantum advantage can be realized even with limited 
hardware. Furthermore, the development of robust quantum error 
mitigation techniques, rather than full error correction, will be crucial to 
improve the reliability of quantum computations without incurring 
excessive overhead, allowing for more stable and dependable SOH and 
RUL predictions.
Concurrently, efforts must focus on seamless integration strategies, 
including the development of standardized quantum software develop
ment kits (SDKs) and APIs that enable straightforward interfacing be
tween classical BMS components and cloud-based quantum computing 
resources. This would allow the computationally intensive quantum 
aspects of PI-QML, such as model training and complex uncertainty 
quantification (QB-PINs), to be offloaded to remote quantum data cen
ters, while the BMS handles real-time data acquisition and classical 
inference. For on-device implementation, hybrid quantum-classical 
frameworks should be designed where the most demanding computa
tions leverage quantum processing units (QPUs) as accelerators for 
specific tasks (e.g., high-dimensional feature encoding or complex 
optimization), while the majority of the processing remains classical. 
This iterative approach will bridge the gap between theoretical quantum 
potential and practical BMS requirements, ensuring that PI-QML can 
incrementally enhance battery prognostics as quantum hardware 
matures.
