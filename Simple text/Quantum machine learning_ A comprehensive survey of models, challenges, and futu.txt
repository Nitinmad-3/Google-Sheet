This survey presents a structured and accessible review of Quantum Machine Learning (QML), 
with the aim of introducing new readers to its fundamental concepts while also covering recent 
research developments. We review key paradigms including Quantum Neural Networks (QNNs), 
Quantum Convolutional Neural Networks (QCNNs), and hybrid quantum-classical architectures. 
Quantum Machine Learning (QML) is an emerging ﬁeld that combines quantum computing and 
machine learning, leveraging qubits that can exist in superposition and exhibit entanglement 
to enable computational capabilities beyond those of classical systems. These properties allow 
quantum models to explore solution spaces in parallel, potentially oﬀering advantages in speed 
or eﬃciency for certain tasks. We analyze theoretical beneﬁts, practical limitations, and emerging 
applications of QML in areas such as medical AI, cryptography, and optimization. Furthermore, 
we discuss key challenges related to quantum hardware scalability, noise resilience, and training 
ineﬃciencies. The paper concludes with a roadmap for future research, emphasizing progress in 
error mitigation, quantum data encoding, and hybrid deep learning integration.
1.  Introduction
1.1.  Background and motivation
Quantum computing has emerged as a transformative computational paradigm that exploits fundamental quantum mechanical 
principles to address problems that are intractable for classical computers [1]. The potential for exponential or polynomial speedups 
oﬀered by quantum algorithms has attracted signiﬁcant attention in domains such as cryptography, optimization, and scientiﬁc 
simulations. In parallel, the rapid growth of artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) has led to increasingly complex 
deep learning models, which face inherent limitations when processing high-dimensional data using classical hardware [2].
Quantum Machine Learning (QML) seeks to bridge these two ﬁelds by integrating quantum computing principles-superposition,
entanglement, and quantum parallelism-into classical ML pipelines. This integration has resulted in the development of novel 
learning paradigms, including Quantum Neural Networks (QNNs), Quantum Convolutional Neural Networks (QCNNs), and hybrid 
quantum–classical architectures, which aim to enhance feature representation, optimization eﬃciency, and model expressivity [3]. 
As a result, QML has emerged as a promising research direction for addressing computational bottlenecks in modern deep learning.

1.2.  Problem statement and research questions

• Computational Eﬃciency: How do QNNs and QCNNs compare with classical deep learning models in terms of accuracy, eﬃ-
ciency, and scalability?
• Hybrid Quantum–Classical Models: What advantages do hybrid architectures oﬀer over purely classical or fully quantum ap-

• Hardware Constraints: How do current quantum hardware limitations inﬂuence the design, training, and deployment of QML

• Application Domains: Which application areas, such as healthcare, ﬁnance, and cybersecurity, are most amenable to near-term

2.  Background and fundamentals
2.1.  Overview of quantum computing
Quantum computing is a computational paradigm that exploits quantum mechanical principles to process information in ways 
that are fundamentally diﬀerent from classical computing [4]. Unlike classical bits, which represent information as binary states, 
quantum bits (qubits) can exist in superpositions of |0⟩ and |1⟩, enabling quantum systems to encode and process information in 
higher-dimensional state spaces [4].
Quantum computation is enabled by core physical phenomena such as superposition, entanglement, and quantum interference, 
which together allow certain quantum algorithms to achieve exponential or polynomial speedups over their classical counterparts in 
speciﬁc problem settings [6]. These properties form the theoretical foundation upon which Quantum Machine Learning models are 
constructed.
2.1.1.  Key concepts in quantum computing
• Qubits: The fundamental unit of quantum information, capable of existing in a superposition of the |0⟩ and |1⟩ basis states (Fig. 1) 
[7].
• Superposition: A quantum property that allows qubits to represent multiple states simultaneously, enabling parallel state evolu-
tion [8].
• Entanglement: A non-classical correlation between qubits in which the state of one qubit is intrinsically linked to the state of 
another, independent of spatial separation [1].
• Quantum Gates and Circuits: Quantum operations, such as Hadamard, CNOT, and Pauli gates, that manipulate qubit states 
within quantum circuits, analogous to logic gates in classical computing [9].
2.1.2.  Quantum computing models
Quantum computing architectures can be broadly categorized based on how computations are implemented:
• Gate-Based Quantum Computing: Relies on sequences of quantum gates applied to qubits and forms the basis of universal 
quantum computation [1].
• Quantum Annealing: Focuses on solving optimization problems by exploiting quantum tunneling to explore energy landscapes 
[10].
• Measurement-Based Quantum Computing (MBQC): Performs computation through adaptive measurements on highly entangled 
resource states rather than explicit quantum circuits [11].
• Topological Quantum Computing: Encodes information in non-Abelian anyons and uses braiding operations to provide inherent 
error resilience [12].
2.2.  Introduction to quantum machine learning (QML)
Quantum Machine Learning (QML) is an interdisciplinary research area that explores how quantum computing can be leveraged to 
enhance or accelerate classical machine learning algorithms. By utilizing quantum properties such as superposition and entanglement, 
QML aims to improve performance in tasks including optimization, classiﬁcation, and representation learning [2].
Existing QML approaches vary widely in terms of how data is encoded, how quantum circuits are structured, and how learning 
parameters are optimized. These design choices play a central role in determining model performance, scalability, and robustness, 
and form the basis for the comparative analysis presented later in this survey.

2.2.3.  Theoretical foundations and complexity of quantum machine learning
Many works in Quantum Machine Learning (QML) motivate performance improvements by referring to the potential exponential or 
polynomial speedups of quantum algorithms. However, such advantages are highly problem-dependent and require careful theoretical 
qualiﬁcation. This subsection provides a concise discussion of the computational foundations underlying QML models, with emphasis 
on complexity considerations.
From a theoretical perspective, quantum advantage in machine learning arises from the ability of quantum systems to represent 
and manipulate information in exponentially large Hilbert spaces using a linear number of qubits. Data encoding strategies such as 
amplitude encoding can, in principle, embed a classical feature vector of dimension 𝑁 into (log 𝑁) qubits, enabling certain linear 
algebra operations to be performed more eﬃciently than their classical counterparts. This advantage, however, assumes eﬃcient 
state preparation, which may itself incur non-trivial computational cost.
Variational Quantum Circuits (VQCs), which form the backbone of Quantum Neural Networks (QNNs) and Quantum Convolutional 
Neural Networks (QCNNs), are typically trained using hybrid quantum–classical optimization loops. While the forward evaluation 
of quantum circuits scales polynomially with the number of qubits and circuit depth, training complexity is dominated by repeated 
circuit executions required for gradient estimation. As a result, QNN training complexity is often comparable to, or higher than, 
classical models in near-term settings.
Theoretical speedups have been formally established for speciﬁc quantum machine learning algorithms, such as quantum kernel 
methods and quantum linear system solvers, under assumptions of eﬃcient data access and low noise. In contrast, general-purpose 
QNNs and QCNNs currently lack formal proofs of exponential advantage over classical deep learning models. Existing evidence pri-
marily demonstrates potential gains in expressivity, parameter eﬃciency, or sample eﬃciency rather than asymptotic computational 
complexity improvements.
Overall, while QML models oﬀer promising theoretical foundations, especially for structured or low-data regimes, their claimed 
speedups should be interpreted as conditional and task-speciﬁc. A rigorous separation between classical and quantum learning com-
plexity remains an open research challenge.
2.3.  Quantum neural networks (QNNs)
Quantum Neural Networks (QNNs) represent a class of QML models inspired by classical artiﬁcial neural networks, in which 
trainable quantum circuits replace or augment classical neuron-based architectures [3]. By leveraging quantum state evolution and 
measurement, QNNs aim to learn expressive representations within quantum Hilbert spaces.
2.3.1.  Architecture of quantum neural networks
A typical QNN architecture consists of:
• Quantum Data Encoding Layer: Encodes classical inputs into quantum states.
• Parameterized Quantum Layers: Applies trainable quantum gates to transform encoded states.
• Measurement and Output Layer: Measures quantum states and maps outcomes to classical outputs.
2.3.2.  Training challenges in QNNs
While QNNs oﬀer theoretical advantages, they introduce unique training challenges:
• Barren Plateau Problem: Gradients may vanish exponentially with circuit depth, hindering eﬃcient training [15].
• Quantum Noise Sensitivity: QNN performance is highly sensitive to decoherence and noise, necessitating error mitigation strate-
gies [16].

2.4.  Hybrid quantum–classical deep learning models
Due to current hardware and scalability limitations, many practical QML approaches adopt hybrid quantum–classical architectures, 
where quantum circuits are embedded within classical deep learning pipelines. These models aim to balance quantum expressivity 
with classical robustness.
2.4.1.  Hybrid model architectures
Common hybrid architectures include:
• Quantum-Assisted Neural Networks (QANNs): Incorporate quantum layers into classical neural networks for enhanced feature 
transformation [17].
• Quantum Variational Autoencoders (QVAEs): Employ quantum circuits for latent space representation learning [13].
• Hybrid Quantum–Classical Convolutional Neural Networks (H-QCNNs): Combine classical convolutional layers with quantum 
pooling or feature extraction stages [18].
3.  Literature review on quantum neural networks (QNNs), quantum convolutional neural networks (QCNNs), and hybrid 
models
Quantum Machine Learning (QML) has emerged as a rapidly growing research ﬁeld that seeks to enhance classical deep learning 
models by exploiting quantum computational principles. As introduced in Section 2, QML integrates concepts such as Variational 
Quantum Circuits (VQCs), quantum data encoding strategies, and hybrid quantum–classical learning architectures. This section 
reviews recent publicly available research on QNNs, QCNNs, and hybrid QML models, with emphasis on architectural design 
choices, optimization strategies, and application domains.
To address the diversity of existing approaches, the reviewed literature is analyzed not only by model type, but also along key 
methodological dimensions, which enables a more structured comparison across studies.
3.1.  Quantum neural networks (QNNs)
3.1.1.  Architectural advancements in QNNs
QNN architectures have evolved to improve computational eﬃciency and expressive power through careful choices of encoding 
schemes and circuit structures. Nguyen et al. [19] compared qubit encoding and amplitude encoding for image classiﬁcation on 
the MNIST dataset, reporting higher accuracy for amplitude encoding (98.9%) compared to classical CNNs (98.2%), albeit with 
increased computational overhead.
Experimental context and benchmark details:
The accuracy improvements reported for amplitude encoding should be interpreted in the context of controlled benchmark 
settings. In the study by Ranga et al. [20], experiments were conducted on the MNIST handwritten digit dataset, focusing on 
low-dimensional subsets and binary classiﬁcation tasks rather than full-scale image recognition. The quantum model employed 
a hybrid quantum–classical architecture, where classical convolutional layers were followed by a parameterized quantum circuit 
operating on a small number of qubits.
Amplitude encoding was evaluated using simulated quantum backends, with circuit execution performed on noiseless quantum 
simulators rather than physical quantum hardware. The reported accuracy of 98.9% was obtained under carefully tuned hyperparam-
eters, including shallow circuit depth, limited qubit counts, and classical pre-processing. In comparison, the classical CNN baseline 
achieved 98.2% accuracy under similar dataset constraints.
These results highlight that reported performance gains reﬂect controlled experimental conditions and architectural choices, rather 
than a generalizable quantum advantage for large-scale image classiﬁcation tasks.
Reese et al. [21] introduced quantum data re-uploading, demonstrating that repeated encoding of classical inputs within shallow 
circuits can signiﬁcantly improve expressivity, achieving a 30% reduction in training data requirements. These studies illustrate 
the strong dependency of QNN performance on encoding and circuit design choices.
3.1.2.  Optimization and training challenges
Training QNNs remains challenging due to barren plateaus and gradient instability. Reese et al. [21] evaluated quantum natural 
gradient descent (QNGD), showing improved training stability compared to standard gradient descent. However, scalability remains 
constrained by circuit depth, noise sensitivity, and hardware limitations. These optimization challenges are examined in detail in 
Section 4.

Despite promising results, QCNNs face scalability challenges due to increased circuit depth and quantum resource requirements.

3.3.  Hybrid quantum–classical models
3.3.1.  Performance comparisons with classical models
Hybrid QML models integrate quantum feature extraction within classical deep learning pipelines, oﬀering a practical compro-
mise between expressivity and scalability. Ranga et al. [24] demonstrated that hybrid QNN–CNN architectures achieved 99.1% 
accuracy on benchmark datasets, outperforming both standalone QNNs and purely classical CNNs.
3.3.2.  Future research in hybrid models
As discussed in Section 5.1, hybrid architectures currently represent the most viable approach for near-term QML deployment. 
Future work should focus on improving quantum–classical data integration, reducing communication overhead, and optimizing 
hybrid feature extraction pipelines.

4.  Key challenges & open problems in quantum machine learning
Quantum Machine Learning (QML) has demonstrated promising theoretical advantages across domains such as computer vision, 
optimization, and quantum cryptography. However, the practical realization of these advantages remains limited by constraints in 
quantum hardware, training dynamics, and model scalability. This section systematically analyzes the key challenges that currently 
hinder QML progress and outlines open research problems that must be addressed for real-world deployment.
4.1.  Hardware constraints in the NISQ era
Current quantum computing platforms operate in the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) regime, which imposes funda-
mental limitations on QML model design and execution. These constraints primarily aﬀect circuit depth, model expressivity, and 
training stability:
• Limited Qubit Availability: Most commercially accessible quantum processors, including IBM Quantum and Google Sycamore, 
operate with fewer than 100 qubits. This restricts the dimensionality of quantum representations and limits the feasibility of 
large-scale QML architectures [4].
• Quantum Noise and Decoherence: Physical qubits suﬀer from decoherence and gate-level noise, leading to unstable model 
outputs and degraded learning performance, particularly in deep quantum circuits [2].
• Circuit Depth Restrictions: Error accumulation constrains the allowable depth of quantum circuits, reducing the expressive 
capacity of deep Quantum Neural Networks (QNNs) and Quantum Convolutional Neural Networks (QCNNs) [3].
These hardware limitations remain a primary bottleneck for executing expressive and noise-resilient QML models, highlighting 
the need for error mitigation strategies and hardware-aware circuit design.

4.2.  Optimization challenges in training QNNs and QCNNs
Beyond hardware constraints, QML models face intrinsic optimization challenges that limit eﬀective training and convergence:
• Barren Plateau Phenomenon: As quantum circuit depth increases, loss landscapes tend to ﬂatten, causing gradients to vanish 
and rendering gradient-based optimization ineﬀective [2].
• Gradient Noise and Instability: Quantum optimization methods, including Quantum Natural Gradient Descent (QNGD), are 
sensitive to stochastic noise introduced by ﬁnite sampling and hardware errors, leading to unstable updates [3].
• High Evaluation Cost: Training QML models requires repeated quantum circuit executions to estimate expectation values, re-
sulting in substantial computational overhead and slow convergence [3].
These optimization issues motivate ongoing research into shallow circuit architectures, alternative loss formulations, and hybrid 
training strategies that mitigate gradient degradation.
4.3.  Scalability and data-related challenges
Scaling QML models beyond proof-of-concept demonstrations remains an unresolved challenge due to both data and system-level 
constraints:
• Quantum Memory Limitations: Unlike classical accelerators such as GPUs and TPUs, quantum hardware lacks eﬃcient memory 
mechanisms for storing and reusing large-scale datasets [4].
• Absence of Standardized Quantum Datasets: The lack of large, widely accepted quantum datasets comparable to ImageNet 
impedes fair benchmarking and reproducibility across QML studies [5].

5.  Future research directions in quantum machine learning
Although Quantum Machine Learning (QML) has demonstrated compelling theoretical potential, it remains at an early stage of 
maturity. Persistent challenges related to hardware constraints, optimization stability, scalability, and real-world deployment continue 
to limit its practical impact. This section outlines key research directions that are likely to shape the evolution of QML, with emphasis 
on hybrid architectures, training strategies, benchmarking standards, and emerging application domains.
5.1.  Advancing hybrid quantum–classical architectures
Given the constraints of current quantum hardware, hybrid quantum–classical models represent the most viable pathway for near-
term QML deployment. Future research should focus on designing architectures that strategically allocate learning tasks between 
quantum and classical components:
• Quantum-Assisted Deep Learning (QADL): Develop architectures in which quantum circuits specialize in feature extraction, 
kernel learning, or optimization, while classical neural networks perform high-level representation learning and decision-making 
[5].
• Layer-Wise Quantum Integration: Introduce selective quantum layers within classical deep learning pipelines to minimize quan-
tum resource usage while retaining potential quantum advantages [2].
• Quantum Graph Neural Networks (QGNNs): Extend hybrid QML frameworks to graph-structured data using quantum-enhanced 
graph convolution mechanisms, enabling applications in complex networks and bioinformatics [3].
5.2.  Improving training and optimization strategies
Optimization remains a central bottleneck in QML, particularly for deep and parameterized quantum circuits. Addressing these 
challenges requires both algorithmic and system-level innovations:
• Quantum-Speciﬁc Optimization Methods: Develop and reﬁne quantum-aware optimizers, such as Quantum Natural Gradient 
Descent (QNGD) and Fisher information-based learning approaches, to stabilize training dynamics [3].
• Hybrid Pre-Training and Quantum Fine-Tuning: Leverage classical deep learning for large-scale pre-training, followed by 
quantum ﬁne-tuning to enhance expressivity and generalization [31].
• Noise-Adaptive Training Techniques: Design adaptive training strategies that dynamically modify circuit depth or parameters 
in response to hardware noise characteristics [16].
5.3.  Enhancing scalability of QML models
Scaling QML beyond small, proof-of-concept tasks remains an open research challenge. Future eﬀorts should target both data 
eﬃciency and system scalability:

• Quantum Data Compression and Autoencoders: Utilize Quantum Autoencoders (QAEs) to reduce the dimensionality of high-
dimensional classical data before quantum processing [13].
• Quantum Federated Learning (QFL): Enable distributed quantum training across multiple nodes, reducing centralized data 
storage and improving privacy [10].
• Emerging Quantum Hardware Paradigms: Investigate scalable platforms such as photonic quantum computing, which oﬀer 
higher connectivity and improved fault tolerance [4].
5.4.  Establishing standardized benchmarks and evaluation protocols
The absence of standardized benchmarks remains a major barrier to fair and reproducible QML research. Addressing this gap is 
critical for meaningful performance comparison:
• Open-Source QML Datasets: Develop publicly available, task-speciﬁc datasets analogous to ImageNet for QML research [27].
• Uniﬁed Benchmarking Frameworks: Deﬁne consistent evaluation protocols that account for accuracy, resource usage, and noise 
robustness [15].
• Hardware-Aware Performance Metrics: Establish benchmarking standards that explicitly consider quantum hardware con-
straints and execution ﬁdelity [5].
5.5.  Emerging and next-generation QML applications
As quantum hardware matures, QML is expected to expand into new application domains that beneﬁt from quantum-enhanced 
computation:
• Quantum Reinforcement Learning (QRL): Apply QML techniques to sequential decision-making tasks in robotics, control sys-
tems, and optimization [10].
• Quantum AI for Drug Discovery: Leverage quantum-enhanced learning models to simulate molecular interactions and accelerate 
pharmaceutical research [32].
• Quantum Adversarial Machine Learning (QAML): Develop quantum-aware defenses against adversarial attacks, strengthening 
AI security in critical systems [16].
5.6.  Research roadmap for the next decade
Based on current trends and technological projections, a high-level roadmap for QML research can be outlined as follows:
• Short-Term (1–3 Years): Focus on noise mitigation, hybrid architectures, and the establishment of standardized benchmarking 
practices.
• Mid-Term (3–7 Years): Develop scalable QML models leveraging 1,000+ qubit systems and expand applications in domains such 
as bioinformatics and cybersecurity.
• Long-Term (7–10 Years): Achieve practical quantum advantage, enable enterprise-scale QML deployment, and advance toward 
fully quantum-native AI architectures.
6.  Conclusion
Quantum Machine Learning (QML) represents a rapidly evolving intersection of quantum computing and artiﬁcial intelligence, 
oﬀering new perspectives on computational eﬃciency, feature representation, and optimization. Over the past decade, substantial 
progress has been made in the development of Quantum Neural Networks (QNNs), Quantum Convolutional Neural Networks (QC-
NNs), and hybrid quantum–classical architectures, with emerging applications in areas such as healthcare, cybersecurity, scientiﬁc 
discovery, and ﬁnance.
This survey has provided a structured and accessible overview of the QML landscape, consolidating foundational concepts, ar-
chitectural developments, and application-driven research. Rather than introducing new algorithms or theoretical frameworks, the 
contribution of this work lies in synthesizing existing literature, organizing QML models across key dimensions, and critically exam-
ining current limitations and research trends.
Despite encouraging results, signiﬁcant challenges continue to impede the practical deployment of QML systems. Limitations 
in quantum hardware, including noise, decoherence, and restricted scalability, constrain model expressivity and training stability. 
In parallel, optimization diﬃculties, data ineﬃciency, and the absence of standardized benchmarks complicate fair evaluation and 
reproducibility. Addressing these challenges is essential for transitioning QML from experimental demonstrations to reliable, real-
world solutions.
6.1.  Key takeaways
• Hybrid QML Models Represent the Most Viable Near-Term Approach: While fully quantum deep learning remains constrained 
by current hardware, hybrid quantum–classical architectures consistently demonstrate improved practicality and performance 
across multiple domains.

• Noise and Optimization Remain Central Challenges: Quantum noise, barren plateaus, and unstable gradients signiﬁcantly

6.2.  Future outlook
QML is currently at a pivotal stage, transitioning from theoretical exploration toward application-oriented research. Continued 
advancements in quantum hardware, hybrid model design, and quantum-speciﬁc optimization techniques are expected to gradually 
close the gap between theoretical promise and practical utility.
As quantum technologies mature, QML has the potential to enable transformative advances across domains ranging from drug 
discovery and secure communications to autonomous decision-making systems. Achieving this vision will require close collaboration 
between quantum physicists, machine learning researchers, and industry practitioners to ensure that future QML systems are both 
technically sound and application-ready.
