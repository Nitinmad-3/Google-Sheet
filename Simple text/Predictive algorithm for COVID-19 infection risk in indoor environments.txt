After the onset of the global COVID-19 pandemic, the deep connections between environmental 
factors and the transmission of airborne infectious diseases (including COVID-19) has become an area 
of relevant scientific and social interest. Indoor environments, where we spend a significant part of our 
daily lives, play a crucial role in shaping the dynamics of disease spread. The mitigation of infection 
risk related to poor indoor air quality and its link with the transmission of airborne diseases has 
emerged as a focal point for research and intervention strategies. This paper presents the results of a 
specific collaborative project in this field, focused on the utilization of Internet of Things (IoT) devices 
for comprehensive indoor environmental monitoring and infectious risk forecasting. In the frame of 
developing effective countermeasures for COVID-19 and future pandemic preparedness, our primary 
goal was to develop a predictive model for infection risk in indoor environments. Parameters such 
as humidity, temperature, CO2, and particulate matter concentrations (namely PM10 and PM2.5), 
have been assessed and modelled as indicators of indoor air quality, with these measures having 
been combined to generate a predictive algorithm specifically able to provide information about 
the transmission dynamics of COVID-19 and airborne infectious diseases within indoor spaces. This 
newly-developed Algorithm for the Prediction of Risk of Infections (APRI) relies on rigorous analyses 
and established different risk thresholds based on temperature, humidity, and CO2 levels. The model 
showed significant associations between environmental factors, such as temperature, CO2 levels, 
humidity, and particulate matter concentrations. A pivotal role of PM10 and PM2.5 in shaping air 
quality in indoor environments has been highlighted, as low PM concentrations corresponded in our 
predictive model to a minimal risk of airborne infectious diseases, while medium or high PM levels were 
associated with variations in temperature, humidity, and CO2 levels, thus corresponding to an elevated 
risk of infection, particularly in the frame of highly diffusive diseases like COVID-19.

cinemas, and museums7. Key factors contributing to indoor pollution include overcrowding, the use of low-
quality fuels for domestic heating, cooking practices, and the use of chemical products for personal hygiene

State of the art and research gap
The air quality we breathe is vital to our health and well-being7. While outdoor air pollution has long been 
acknowledged as a major public health issue, there has been a growing focus on indoor air quality (IAQ) in recent 
years. People spend up to 90% of their time indoors33, where pollutant concentrations can often surpass outdoor 
levels due to factors such as poor ventilation, building materials, and human activities34. These pollutants, 
including particulate matter (PM), volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and carbon dioxide (CO2), have been 
associated with various health issues, ranging from respiratory diseases and allergies to cardiovascular conditions 
and even cognitive impairments35. In addition to traditional pollutants such as PM2.5 and CO₂, recent studies 
have drawn attention to the widespread presence of indoor microplastics and microfibers, which may pose 
emerging health risks through inhalation and long-term exposure36. The emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic 
has heightened concerns about IAQ and its potential impact on disease transmission. Initially, the focus was 
on surface and droplet transmission; however, mounting evidence indicates that airborne transmission plays a 
significant role in spreading SARS-CoV-2, the virus responsible for COVID-19. This highlights the importance 
of maintaining good IAQ to minimize the risk of viral transmission, especially in densely populated indoor 
settings such as schools, offices, and public transportation37. Traditionally, IAQ assessment relied on expensive, 
fixed monitoring stations. While these stations provide valuable insights into regional air quality, their high 
installation and maintenance costs limit their numbers, often resulting in a coarse-grained overview that leaves 
many areas without direct measurements38. This limitation hinders the development of targeted interventions 
and personalized solutions. In response, the advent of low-cost, portable sensors39 has opened new avenues for 
more widespread and detailed IAQ monitoring. These sensors are capable of measuring pollutants like PM, CO2, 
and VOCs, offering real-time data from a variety of locations at a fraction of the cost40. A notable example is the 
increasing deployment of low-cost wireless sensors in public transport vehicles such as buses, trams, and trains. 
This strategy offers broader spatial coverage compared to stationary monitoring stations, although the data is 
limited to the routes covered by these vehicles41,42. This shift has led to the rise of citizen-science initiatives, where 
individuals use personal sensors to contribute data, generating large datasets that help map spatial and temporal 
variations in IAQ. Crowdsourcing represents a promising approach to enhancing air quality monitoring. By 
equipping individuals with portable and affordable sensors, it is possible to gather high-resolution air quality 
data from a wide range of locations. Several projects have investigated this method, demonstrating its benefits 
for individual citizens and the feasibility of crowd-based data collection. Nonetheless, further research is needed 
to transform these solutions into sustainable long-term monitoring strategies43–46. Nevertheless, challenges 
persist regarding sensor accuracy, calibration, and data standardization. Moreover, citizen-science initiatives 
require robust methodologies to ensure data quality and reliability. While promising, further studies are

transmission remains incomplete. Existing research often relies on limited data from specific environments, 
and knowledge gaps exist regarding the combined effects of various pollutants and their interactions with 
environmental factors. Additionally, existing predictive models require further refinement to achieve sufficient 
accuracy and interpretability for real- world applications. This study addresses these gaps by developing a novel 
predictive model for COVID-19 infection risk based on the quality of indoor environments. In collaboration 
with a manufacturer of indoor environmental monitoring devices, we leverage their advanced sensors capable 
of collecting various parameters including relative humidity, temperature, sound pressure level, PM10, PM2.5, 
CO2, and NO2.
Our research aims to achieve the following:
•	 • Develop a comprehensive predictive model for COVID-19 infection risk across diverse indoor environ
ments. This model will encompass residential, commercial, and public enclosed spaces, including scenarios 
such as hospital waiting rooms, university corridors, company front offices, commercial and corporate offices.
•	 • Identify key parameters and indicators that characterize the indoor environment and its associated COV

on transmission risk.
•	 • Demonstrate the pivotal role of indoor air quality monitoring in reducing the overall environmental micro
bial load. By evaluating parameters like ventilation system effectiveness and sanitation procedures, we aim to 
highlight the importance of maintaining healthy indoor environments.
•	 • Provide valuable insights on how to mitigate the risk of COVID-19 transmission across various enclosed 
spaces. We expect the findings from this study to inform evidence-based strategies for improving indoor air 
quality and reducing virus spread.
Specific activities within this study include:
•	 • Analyzing a large dataset collected in diverse indoor scenarios. This data will be characterized in terms of air 
pollution/contamination to determine the quality of indoor air based on parameters sensitive to Coronavirus 
spread.
•	 • Developing and validating the predictive model. We will utilize advanced machine learning techniques to 
create a model that is both accurate and interpretable for practical implementation.
•	 • Evaluating the model’s performance in real-world settings. Collaborating with public health agencies and 
stakeholders, we will test the model’s effectiveness in different indoor environments.
Data workflow: gathering and processing data
In order to best prepare for the implementation of any algorithm, it is essential to be able to qualify the data. 
The answers provided will serve as a basis to define the real needs for its implementation: once all the answers 
are gathered, then the project as a whole is defined according to: deployment, required autonomy, number of 
sensors, type of network sought (high-speed, low-speed, private, operated etc.,), expected level of security. The 
whole framework’s pipeline is composed of several separate blocks that make configuration easy and follow the 
classic data science pipeline (Fig. 1).
The first block is the appropriate configuration of the IoT sensors chosen to collect the data. The configuration 
can be done sensor by sensor or on batches of products using dedicated tools, locally or remotely. They are small 
smart devices with a smooth, white surface, small size and simple, straightforward design. Small slots on all 
sides allow continuous ventilation for effective air quality measurement. Due to their cubic shape, these portable 
sensors can be placed without the need for support, for example on shelves and tables. The devices can connect 
to the collection server (cloud or intranet) via WiFi or NB-IoT connectivity. Upon request it can support wireless 
connectivity such as LTE-4G, LTE-M, LoRa, LoRa-WAN. They have indicator lights on the top of the case. Based 
on the frequency of illumination and the color, they allow us to understand the current activity and the general 
health status of the device. Audible indicators signal when the device is switched on and off. The second step is 
the data collection from the configured devices: several sensors were placed in the laboratory to measure various 
air quality parameters (HUMIDITY, TEMPERATURE, CO2, CO2EQ, PM10 etc.) at 5-minute intervals. All data 
is sent to the cloud from which daily reports can be downloaded in csv format. Once the data is collected, it then 
enters the data preparation stage. Data preparation or data cleaning is the process of sorting and filtering the raw 
data to remove unnecessary and inaccurate data. Raw data is checked for errors, duplication, miscalculations

In our case, data preparation was carried out using Python notebooks and Microsoft Excel. In addition 
to standard data-cleaning procedures, such as handling missing values and removing outliers, a two-phase 
calibration process was implemented to ensure sensor accuracy. In the initial phase, a set of benchmark 
measurements was collected using certified, higher-precision instruments. These were used to assess and 
adjust the baseline readings of the deployed sensors. In the second phase, a manual calibration was performed 
by calculating parameter-specific offsets for each sensor. This was done by comparing multiple observations 
from different sensors co-located in the same environment, allowing us to correct for inter-sensor variability. 
Furthermore, an exploratory analysis was conducted using additional observations to evaluate the performance 
and consistency of the devices. This approach ensured a higher level of data consistency and measurement 
reliability throughout the observation period. The work presented in this paper is based on the data collected 
from January 2020 and January 2022, with a total of about 35,000 observations. Data were gathered from 
primary, elementary, and secondary schools located in various cities across the province of Lecce, in southern 
Italy. This area was selected due to the logistical feasibility of sensor installation and monitoring. The cities 
represent diverse environmental and structural conditions, and sensors were placed in different locations within 
classrooms, such as near windows, next to doors, and in other representative positions, to capture a wide range 
of indoor environmental dynamics.

Labeling the dataset
To construct a usable dataset for supervised learning, it was necessary to first assign labels to the collected 
observations, as the raw data consisted solely of environmental measurements (e.g., temperature, humidity) 
without any predefined indicators of COVID-19 transmission risk. To address this, we employed an unsupervised 
learning approach, K-means clustering, to infer risk levels based on environmental patterns. The k-means 
method aims to partition the points into k groups such that the sum of squares from points to the assigned 
cluster centers is minimized. At the minimum, all cluster centers are at the mean of the set of data points which 
are nearest to the cluster center. Typically, to process the learning data, the K-means algorithm in data mining 
starts with a first group of randomly selected centroids, which are used as the beginning points for every cluster, 
and then performs iterative (repetitive) calculations to optimize the positions of the centroids. It halts creating 
and optimizing clusters when either: The centroids have stabilized — there is no change in their values because 
the clustering has been successful. The defined number of iterations has been achieved. For our algorithm, 
centroids were chosen based on certain thresholds as referred in the study carried out during the COVID-19 
pandemic by Di Gilio et al.2 to identify specific CO2 thresholds associated to low, medium and high-risk of 
COVID-19 spread in indoor environments. The thresholds are based on temperature, humidity and CO2 levels 
and were identified as described in Fig. 3.
Then, by initializing the algorithm’s centroids based on the chosen thresholds, the 35,000 observations are 
divided and clustered into three groups. A distribution emerges that can be seen in the 2D graphs reported in 
Fig. 4 as a function of temperature and humidity, temperature and CO2.The distribution would be much clearer 
and sharper using a 3D graph where the three parameters used to define the assessment thresholds are visible. 
An interesting factor of the clustering process, is the emergent relationship between the identified risk clusters 
and particulate matter concentrations (PM10 and PM2,5), which were not explicitely included in the initial 
threshold definition. This enforces findings from previous literature that indicate a synergistic effect between 
poor air quality and increased viral transmission potential.
A low concentration level of PM (typically below 5 µg/m3) belongs to the cluster identified as posing a low 
risk to air health (Fig. 5). A medium and high level are concentrated in the range with values above 5, depending 
on the associated parameters of temperature, humidity and CO2.

Apply ML techniques: inference with regression
After labeling the dataset through clustering, the next step is to enable prediction of risk levels for new, unseen 
observations. While clustering groups existing data into discrete categories, it does not provide a way to estimate 
risk continuously or to infer risk on future measurements. To address this, supervised machine learning 
regression techniques are applied to learn the relationship between environmental parameters and the assigned 
risk labels. The goal is to develop a predictive model capable of estimating a risk score for any new observation 
based on its sensor data, allowing real-time assessment and more nuanced risk evaluation beyond fixed clusters.
Regression is considered one of the most common Machine Learning (ML) tasks. There are various 
approaches, for example, using a standalone model of the Linear Regression or the Decision Tree. But in general, 
a single decision tree could not be enough, and results could not be very stable and reliable. The idea is to make 
use of ensemble learning: it is a training on multiple ML algorithms and a combination of their predictions in 
some way. Such an approach tends to make more accurate predictions than any individual model. An Ensemble 
model is a model that consists of many base models. As commonly said, ‘Forest is better than a single tree’: 
therefore, we choose to implement a Random Forest algorithm in our analysis. Random forest consists of many 
individual decision trees that operate as an ensemble. Each individual tree in the random forest spits out a 
class prediction and the class with the most votes becomes our model’s prediction. While some trees may be 
wrong, many other trees will be right, so as a group the trees are able to move in the correct direction. Random 
Forest limits the greatest disadvantage of Decision Trees. It almost does not overfit due to subset and feature 
randomization. Firstly, it uses a unique subset of the initial data for every base model which helps to make 
Decision Trees less correlated. Secondly, it splits each node in every Decision Tree using a random set of features. 
Such an approach means that no single tree sees all the data, which helps to focus on the general patterns within 
the training data and reduces sensitivity to noise. The choice of Random Forest was motivated not only by its 
performance but also by its robustness to noise, ability to handle non-linear relationships, and capability to 
assess feature importance. Moreover, it supports rapid training and inference even on moderately large datasets. 
To ensure transparency and reproducibility, we carefully selected the hyperparameters based on a grid search 
with cross-validation. The final model was configured with:
•	 Number of trees (n_estimators): 100, to ensure stability without incurring excessive computational cost;
•	 Maximum depth (max_depth): set to 10 to control overfitting and balance bias-variance;
•	 Minimum samples per split (min_samples_split): 2;
•	 Number of features considered at each split (max_features): sqrt, the default setting for regression tasks, to 
maintain model diversity.

Comparison with other algorithms
Before choosing the Random Forest as the algorithm for making inferences on the new data, we conducted a 
comprehensive comparison of multiple machine learning models to evaluate their performance on the available 
dataset. The selected models included widely adopted algorithms in the literature for regression tasks: Decision 
Tree Regressor, XGBoost Regressor (XGB), and CatBoost Regressor, in addition to Random Forest. Each model’s 
predictive performance was assessed based on key statistical indicators: median, mean, and standard deviation 
of RMSE (Root Mean Square Error). To better visualize error distributions and variability, we also employed 
box plots (see Fig. 8). Although all models demonstrated reasonably good average performance, Random Forest 
consistently yielded the most stable and accurate predictions, with lower error variability and fewer extreme 
values. The XGBoost Regressor, despite being widely recognized for its strong performance on structured/
tabular data, underperformed in our context. This can be attributed to several factors. First, XGBoost is highly

Discussion and conclusions
This study investigated the potential of machine learning models to predict COVID-19 transmission risk based 
on indoor air quality parameters collected by Internet of Things (IoT) sensors. By comparing various algorithms, 
the study identified a Random Forest model that achieved the best performance in predicting risk based on 
temperature, humidity, CO2, and PM concentrations. This finding highlights the strong correlation between air 
quality and potential COVID-19 transmission, supporting previous research suggesting that specific pollutants 
and environmental factors can influence viral survival and spread49.
It is important to clarify that while our results demonstrate a significant correlation between particulate 
matter (PM) concentrations and COVID-19 infection risk, they do not inherently confirm a direct causal 
relationship. Elevated PM levels may serve as proxies or indirect indicators of underlying environmental and 
behavioral conditions that facilitate viral transmission. For example, increased PM levels might reflect poor 
ventilation, higher occupancy densities, or specific indoor activities generating aerosols.

The ability to infer transmission risk based on readily available environmental sensor data offers valuable 
insights for preventive public health strategies, particularly in public spaces and high-risk settings like schools, 
offices and healthcare facilities. Several key strengths contribute to the study’s significance:
	1.	 Real-world data: Utilizing data from actual sensor deployments ensures the model’s applicability to re
al-world scenarios.
	2.	 Comprehensive parameter analysis: The model considers multiple air quality parameters, providing a more 
holistic understanding of the relationship with COVID-19 risk.
	3.	 Robust model selection: Comparing different algorithms ensures the chosen model offers the best perfor
mance and generalizability.
	4.	 Scalability and flexibility: The model’s rapid inference and compatibility with mobile devices facilitate re
al-time monitoring and intervention strategies.
However, some limitations deserve consideration:
•	 Model validation: While the chosen model performed well, further validation with expert input and compar
ison with established metrics could strengthen its reliability.
•	 Limited scope: The study focused on specific parameters and may not capture all potential risk factors. In
vestigation into additional environmental and behavioral factors could further enhance the model’s accuracy.
Our analysis primarily emphasizes environmental parameters; however, it is important to recognize potential 
confounding factors such as human behavior, occupancy density, and ventilation rates. Human activities, such 
as speaking loudly or coughing, occupancy patterns, and the effectiveness of ventilation systems significantly 
influence indoor air quality and consequently the risk of airborne infection transmission. Although our current 
model does not explicitly incorporate these behavioral and occupancy variables due to ethical and logistical 
constraints, their inclusion in future iterations could substantially refine predictions, helping to distinguish 
clearly between environmental conditions and behavioral determinants of infection risk.
Future work
While the current model provides a robust framework for assessing COVID-19 infection risk based on indoor 
environmental parameters, several avenues for future development remain open. First, additional expert input 
from epidemiologists, building engineers, and public health officials will be sought to refine the environmental 
thresholds and validate model generalizability across different regions and seasons. Second, the model could be 
expanded to integrate behavioral data (e.g., room occupancy rates, activity levels) where ethically and legally 
feasible. Third, future versions will aim to include adaptive learning mechanisms, allowing the algorithm to 
recalibrate automatically as new data become available. Furthermore, broader deployment across varied indoor 
settings—such as hospitals, public transportation, and commercial venues—will help stress-test the model’s 
reliability and adaptability. Finally, integration with smart HVAC and ventilation systems may allow the 
algorithm to be used not only for monitoring but also for automated risk mitigation actions in real-time. Future 
research should include controlled studies designed explicitly to differentiate between direct effects of PM on 
virus viability and indirect associations resulting from other co-varying environmental or behavioral factors.
Data privacy/ethics
Throughout the project, strict attention was paid to data privacy and ethical compliance, particularly given the 
involvement of educational institutions and the potential sensitivity of indoor monitoring data. All data were 
anonymized prior to analysis to ensure that no personally identifiable information could be linked to individuals, 
classrooms, or specific schools. The collection and processing of environmental data adhered to relevant GDPR 
guidelines and institutional ethical standards. Furthermore, sensor deployment was carried out with the full 
knowledge and consent of participating schools, and no behavioral or health data from students or staff were 
recorded. The research design prioritized transparency, minimal intrusion, and respect for the privacy of all 
participants, in line with the ethical imperatives of public health research in sensitive environments.
